## H2 outline (each opens with a self-contained, citable claim sentence)

1. **Why routine screening matters in Kenya** — Kenya now carries a double burden of disease: non-communicable conditions like hypertension and diabetes are rising fast with urbanisation, while infectious diseases such as malaria, HIV and typhoid remain a serious and active concern.
2. **The core screening panel for adults in Kenya** — A sensible baseline panel for most Kenyan adults covers blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, a full blood count, HIV testing, and cervical screening for women, with malaria and typhoid testing added where regionally or clinically relevant.
3. **Malaria, HIV, typhoid and cervical cancer: conditions with distinctly Kenyan weight** — Malaria risk in Kenya depends heavily on where you live, HIV testing carries added weight given Kenya's national prevalence, and cervical cancer remains one of the most diagnosed cancers among Kenyan women despite being one of the most preventable.
4. **How often to screen, by age and risk** — Healthy adults in their twenties and thirties do well with a baseline screen and a repeat every one to three years, while people over 40 or with a family history of diabetes, hypertension or cancer should screen more often.
5. **Where and how screening happens in Kenya** — Kenyans can access screening through public health facilities, NHIF/SHA-linked care, private hospitals and laboratories, and increasingly through home-collection services, each with different trade-offs in cost, wait time and convenience.
