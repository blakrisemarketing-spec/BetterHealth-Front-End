## Angle
A urine test is one of the cheapest, fastest tests in medicine and it screens for kidney trouble, urinary infection, diabetes and, in pregnancy, preeclampsia risk, all from one sample. Most patients have seen the dipstick strip but never learned what each line on the panel means or what a "normal" result chart looks like. The hook: this one test does double duty as a routine wellness check and an antenatal screening tool across Africa, and knowing how to read the components (protein, glucose, blood, leukocytes, nitrites, pH, specific gravity) turns a result printout into something a patient can actually use before seeing a doctor.

## H2 outline (each opens with a self-contained citable claim)

1. **What a urine test checks for** — A routine urinalysis screens urine for protein, glucose, blood, leukocytes, nitrites, pH, specific gravity and other markers in a single sample, giving an early window into kidney health, infection, hydration and metabolic status.
2. **How a urine test is done: sample collection and procedure** — A urinalysis needs a small "midstream clean-catch" urine sample collected into a sterile container, and how the sample is collected affects how reliable the result is.
3. **How to read a normal urine test results chart** — Most reference ranges call a urinalysis normal when protein, glucose, blood, leukocytes and nitrites are all negative or trace, urine pH sits between about 4.5 and 8, and specific gravity falls between about 1.005 and 1.030.
4. **Protein in urine: what it can mean** — Protein in urine, called proteinuria, is one of the earliest lab signs that the kidneys' filtering units may be leaking, and it is a marker doctors watch closely in both general checkups and pregnancy.
5. **Glucose in urine: what it can mean** — Glucose is not normally present in urine, so finding it usually means blood sugar has risen high enough to spill past the kidneys' reabsorption limit, most often pointing toward diabetes.
6. **Blood in urine: what it can mean** — Blood in urine, called haematuria, can come from causes as minor as a urinary infection or as significant as kidney stones or, rarely, a tumour, so it is always a flag worth a doctor's review even without pain.
7. **Leukocytes and nitrites in urine: signs of a urinary tract infection** — Leukocytes (white blood cells) and nitrites on a urine test are the two markers most often used together to flag a likely urinary tract infection.
8. **Urine tests in pregnancy: screening for preeclampsia and gestational diabetes** — Routine urine protein and glucose screening is a standard part of antenatal checkups across Africa, used to help flag preeclampsia risk and gestational diabetes early.
9. **Abnormal urine test results: what happens next** — An abnormal result on a single urinalysis is a flag for further testing, not a diagnosis on its own, and a doctor interprets it alongside symptoms, history and sometimes a repeat or confirmatory test.
