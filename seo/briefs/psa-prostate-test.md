## Angle
PSA is one of the most requested but least understood tests men ask for. The hook is twofold: prostate cancer incidence and mortality are widely reported in the literature as disproportionately high in men of African descent, which makes this test genuinely relevant to a Ghanaian and pan-African audience; and at the same time, a raised PSA is a common, mostly non-cancer event (BPH, prostatitis, recent ejaculation, catheterisation, vigorous exercise). The article has to hold both truths at once without tipping into "every man must get tested" (overclaims screening benefit) or "a high number means cancer" (misreads a risk-assessment tool as a diagnostic one). Current mainstream guidance favours an informed, shared decision with a clinician over blanket population screening, because of real overdiagnosis and overtreatment tradeoffs. That nuance is the safety backbone of the piece.

## H2 outline (each opens with a self-contained citable claim)

1. **What a PSA test measures** — PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a protein made by the prostate gland, and the blood test measures how much of it is circulating, not cancer directly.
2. **Why PSA matters more for men of African descent** — Prostate cancer incidence and mortality are widely reported in the epidemiological literature as disproportionately high in men of African descent, which is why family history and ancestry are part of every risk conversation.
3. **Why a raised PSA does not mean cancer** — A raised PSA is common and has several everyday, non-cancer causes, including benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), prostatitis, a recent ejaculation, urinary catheterisation, and vigorous exercise or cycling shortly before the test.
4. **PSA normal range** — PSA is reported in nanograms per millilitre (ng/mL), and what counts as "normal" shifts upward with age rather than sitting at one fixed cutoff.
5. **Should every man get a PSA test?** — Major clinical guidelines do not recommend automatic population-wide PSA screening, because the test can lead to overdiagnosis and overtreatment of slow-growing cancers that may never have caused harm.
6. **Age and family history: the two risk factors that matter most** — Risk rises with age past the mid-40s to 50s, and having a father or brother with prostate cancer roughly doubles a man's own risk.
7. **What happens after a raised PSA result** — A raised result is usually followed by a repeat test, a free-to-total PSA ratio, or a urology referral, not an automatic biopsy.
