## Citable claim per H2
- Lassa fever is a viral illness spread mainly through contact with the urine or droppings of infected Mastomys rats, and it can also pass between people through direct contact with an infected person's blood or body fluids.
- Symptoms of Lassa fever typically start 6 to 21 days after exposure, the incubation period cited by WHO, the CDC and Nigeria's NCDC.
- About 80% of Lassa virus infections are mild or cause no symptoms at all, according to WHO, and the ones that do show up often start with fever and weakness that looks a lot like malaria or typhoid in the first few days.
- Severe Lassa fever can progress to facial swelling, bleeding from the mouth, nose or gums, low blood pressure and, in the worst cases, shock, and these signs need immediate hospital care.
- Lassa fever, malaria and typhoid all start with fever in Nigeria, and a laboratory test, not symptoms alone, is what tells them apart.
- Confirming Lassa fever requires an RT-PCR test carried out at a designated public health laboratory, not a routine walk-in blood test.
- The antiviral ribavirin can help when it is started early in the illness, and WHO puts the overall case-fatality rate at around 1%, rising to as much as 15% among patients hospitalised with severe disease.
- Lassa fever cases in Nigeria rise every dry season, roughly November to April, when Mastomys rats move closer to homes in search of food.
- Recognising Lassa fever symptoms early and getting a fever tested for its common, treatable causes gives a person the best chance of timely, appropriate care.
