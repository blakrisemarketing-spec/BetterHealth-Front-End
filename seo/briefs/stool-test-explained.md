## Angle
A stool test is one of the most requested but least understood lab orders across
Africa: patients are told to "bring a stool sample" with almost no explanation of
what it actually checks for. This is a patients-led explainer that demystifies the
main stool test types (routine microscopy for ova and parasites, occult blood, stool
culture, and antigen tests like H. pylori), walks through the collection procedure
end to end (since poor sample collection is a common, avoidable cause of unclear
results), and gives calibrated, non-alarmist framing on what a positive occult blood
result means (a screening flag, not a diagnosis). Intestinal parasites remain a
significant public-health concern across much of Africa per WHO's neglected tropical
disease framing, so the article leans into that pan-African reality without
Ghana-only framing or invented statistics.

## Citable claim per H2
- A stool test examines a sample of your faeces in a lab to look for parasites, blood, infection, or signs of digestive disease that are not visible to the eye.
- Stool tests fall into a few main types, and which one your doctor orders depends on what they are trying to rule in or out.
- A stool sample collected the wrong way, contaminated with urine or water, or left too long before testing, can produce a misleading result regardless of how good the lab is.
- Most routine stool tests need no fasting and no special diet, though a few less common tests ask you to avoid certain foods or medications beforehand.
- A positive occult blood result means the test detected blood in your stool that was not visible to the naked eye, and it is a screening signal that needs follow-up, not a diagnosis on its own.
- A stool ova and parasites exam looks for the eggs, larvae, or adult forms of intestinal parasites, several of which remain a significant public-health concern across parts of Africa.
- Turnaround time for a stool test depends on what is being tested for, ranging from same-day microscopy to several days for a culture.
- What you do next with a stool test result depends entirely on what it found, and a doctor is the one who decides whether it needs treatment or further testing.
