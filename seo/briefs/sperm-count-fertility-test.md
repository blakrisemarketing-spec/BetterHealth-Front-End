## Search intent

Informational: a reader, usually part of a couple trying to conceive, wants to
understand what a sperm count test actually measures, what a normal result looks
like, and what a low result does or does not mean before deciding whether to see a
doctor or try an at-home kit first.

## Angle

A sperm count test is popularly understood as "counting sperm," but a proper semen
analysis measures far more: concentration, motility (progressive, non-progressive,
immotile), morphology, vitality, volume, pH, and liquefaction, matching BetterHealth's
own "Sperm & Semen Health" catalogue panel. The article leads with what the test
covers, gives WHO's lower reference limits in hedged, non-alarmist terms, and spends
real weight on two clinical-safety points: fertility is a couple-based issue, and one
low result is a prompt to retest, not a diagnosis. At-home kits are covered honestly
as a concentration-only screening tool, not a lab-analysis substitute.

1. **What a sperm count test actually checks** (id: `what-it-checks`)
   "A full semen analysis measures sperm count and concentration, motility, shape
   (morphology), vitality, and several properties of the semen itself, including
   volume, pH, and how quickly the sample liquefies."

2. **How to prepare for a sperm count test** (id: `how-to-prepare`)
   "Most labs ask for a short abstinence period before the sample, commonly
   somewhere between two and seven days, since too little or too much time since
   the last ejaculation can both distort the count and motility results."

3. **Reading your result: what counts as a normal sperm count to get pregnant**
   (id: `reading-your-result`)
   "There is no single number that guarantees a pregnancy, but the World Health
   Organization publishes lower reference limits for semen parameters that labs use
   to flag results as below the typical fertile range."

4. **One low result does not mean infertility** (id: `one-low-result`)
   "A single low sperm count or motility result is not a diagnosis of infertility on
   its own, since semen quality naturally varies from sample to sample and can be
   temporarily affected by illness, fever, heat exposure, alcohol, stress, or certain
   medications."

5. **At-home sperm count test kits versus a lab semen analysis** (id: `at-home-kits`)
   "At-home sperm count test kits can give a rough screening indication of sperm
   concentration, but they are less thorough than a full lab semen analysis, since
   most only measure count and do not assess motility or morphology."

## Ranges source

WHO's widely used lower reference limits (5th percentile) for semen parameters,
presented in hedged, general terms rather than tied to a specific manual edition
number, since labs may still reference different editions. Figures used: volume
1.5 mL or more, concentration 15 million/mL or more, total count 39 million or
more, total motility 40% or more, progressive motility 32% or more, vitality 58%
or more, normal morphology 4% or more, pH 7.2 or higher, liquefaction within 60
minutes. Framed explicitly as floors seen in fertile men, not averages or pass
marks, and readers are told to confirm the range printed on their own lab report.
