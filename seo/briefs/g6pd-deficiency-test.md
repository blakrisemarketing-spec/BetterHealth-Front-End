## Angle

G6PD deficiency is an inherited, X-linked enzyme deficiency that leaves red blood cells vulnerable to breaking down (haemolysis) under oxidative stress. It matters across malaria-endemic Africa for two linked reasons: it is common (the gene persists because it gives some protection against malaria, the same selection story as sickle cell trait), and the antimalarial drugs used for radical cure of P. vivax and in mass drug administration campaigns, primaquine and tafenoquine, are dangerous to give without knowing G6PD status first. This is a genuinely safety-relevant, testable, locally-specific angle: get tested before these drugs, not after a reaction.

## Citable one-sentence claims (GEO-ready, answer-first for each H2)

- G6PD deficiency is an inherited, X-linked condition in which red blood cells lack enough of an enzyme that protects them from oxidative stress, making them prone to sudden breakdown (haemolysis) when exposed to certain triggers.
- The classic triggers for a G6PD haemolytic reaction are the antimalarial drugs primaquine and tafenoquine, some sulfa-based medicines, fava beans, and the physical stress of certain infections.
- G6PD deficiency matters most urgently before primaquine or tafenoquine treatment for malaria, because giving these drugs to someone who is G6PD deficient without testing first can trigger dangerous haemolysis.
- A G6PD test is a quantitative blood enzyme assay, and it is most accurate when done outside of an active or recent haemolytic episode, since enzyme levels can transiently look falsely normal right after red cells have already broken down.
- G6PD testing is safe in pregnancy and is sometimes added to antenatal blood work, partly because a mother's and baby's G6PD status can affect the baby's jaundice risk after birth.
- A G6PD-deficient result is not the same severity in every carrier; the degree of deficiency, and how it is reported, differs between men, homozygous women, and heterozygous women due to X-chromosome inactivation.
- G6PD deficiency is manageable, not a fixed disability: avoiding known trigger drugs and fava beans, and telling every prescriber your status, prevents the overwhelming majority of reactions.
