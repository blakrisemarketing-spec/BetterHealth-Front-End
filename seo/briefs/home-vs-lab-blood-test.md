## Angle

A reader who is already convinced they should get tested is now choosing between
booking a home blood draw and walking into a lab or hospital. The angle is
practical and reassuring: the collection location does not change the lab
science (same accredited lab processes both samples), most common tests travel
well, a short list of tests are better suited to a lab visit, and the real
tradeoffs are convenience, privacy and schedule, not accuracy. Close with a
simple decision framework rather than a hard recommendation, and point to
`/book`, `/pricing` and `/what-we-test` instead of any invented figures.

## H2 outline (each opens with a self-contained citable claim)

1. **What a home blood draw actually involves** — A home blood draw sends a
   trained phlebotomist to collect your sample at your home or office using the
   same needles, tubes and technique used in a walk-in lab.
2. **Does the collection location change the accuracy of your results?** — Your
   sample is processed at the same accredited laboratory whether it was drawn
   at home or at a walk-in clinic, so the lab science behind the result does not
   change.
3. **Which tests are and aren't practical for home draw** — Most routine blood
   tests travel well from a home draw to the lab, but a handful of tests need
   handling, timing or equipment that a walk-in lab is better placed to provide.
4. **Does fasting still apply if the phlebotomist comes to you?** — Fasting
   requirements are set by the test, not by where the needle goes in, so an
   eight to twelve hour fast still applies to a home draw exactly as it would
   at a lab.
5. **Convenience and privacy: what each option actually trades off** — Home
   blood draw trades a queue and a commute for a scheduled visit at your own
   address, which matters most if you have mobility limits, a packed schedule,
   needle anxiety or privacy concerns.
6. **How to decide between a home draw and a lab visit** — The right choice
   usually comes down to which tests you need, your schedule, and your comfort
   with either setting, not which option is "better" in the abstract.

## Clinical-safety notes

- Never claim home draw is "more accurate" or "less accurate" than a lab
  visit; the correct, defensible claim is that both are processed by the same
  accredited lab, so accuracy depends on collection technique and handling,
  not location.
- Name tests that generally need lab-based handling or equipment (for
  example, tests requiring immediate spinning/processing, special
  temperature-controlled transport, or same-day multi-step protocols) without
  fabricating a specific list of BetterHealth's own restricted tests; keep
  language general ("some specialised tests") unless a source confirms
  specifics.
- No invented prices anywhere; point to `/pricing` and `/book`.
