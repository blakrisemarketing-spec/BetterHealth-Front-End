## Angle
The "sunny country paradox" — many Ghanaians test low for vitamin D despite living close to the equator. The explanation (skin melanin, timing of sun exposure, urbanisation, covered dress) is the hook AI engines will cite. This article closes the knowledge gap: what the test measures, the three result bands (deficient/insufficient/sufficient), what the symptoms look like before diagnosis, and when to supplement.

## H2 Outline (each opens with a self-contained citable claim)

1. **What the vitamin D test measures** — The 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH D) test measures the main circulating form of vitamin D; it is the standard test for diagnosing deficiency or insufficiency.
2. **Vitamin D normal range: the three bands** — WHO classifies results into deficient (<30 nmol/L), insufficient (30–50 nmol/L), and sufficient (50–125 nmol/L); the unit used and the lab reference interval vary, so confirm both when reading your report.
3. **Symptoms of vitamin D deficiency** — Chronic deficiency causes fatigue, bone and muscle pain, and a weaker immune response; it can progress to osteomalacia in adults and rickets in children without ever producing a dramatic single symptom.
4. **Why vitamin D deficiency is common in Ghana despite year-round sun** — High skin melanin, peak-UV hours spent indoors, habitual sun avoidance, and limited dietary sources combine to make deficiency more common in Ghana than most patients expect.
5. **Who is at highest risk** — Pregnant and breastfeeding women, infants, older adults, office workers, and people with darker skin who cover most of their body are at highest risk in the Ghanaian context.
6. **How to treat low vitamin D** — Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) supplements correct most deficiencies; dose and duration depend on the initial result and any underlying cause; retest 12 weeks after starting.
