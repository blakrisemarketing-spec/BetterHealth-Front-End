## Angle
A patients-led explainer for someone with a persistent cough, a known contact of a TB
case, or anyone curious what a TB test actually involves. Covers the three families
of TB tests: (1) sputum smear microscopy and GeneXpert/molecular testing, the
standard diagnostic tests for active pulmonary TB, done on a coughed-up sputum
sample, with GeneXpert also detecting rifampicin drug resistance; (2) the
tuberculin skin test (TST/Mantoux) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA),
which detect TB infection, including latent, non-contagious infection, rather than
confirming active disease; and (3) chest X-ray as a supportive imaging tool, not a
standalone diagnostic. Leads with the latent-vs-active distinction since it is the
single most common point of confusion in TB testing. Covers classic active-TB
symptoms (persistent cough over two to three weeks, night sweats, weight loss,
fever) with the hedge that these overlap with many other conditions. Closes with
the TB-HIV syndemic, a well-documented WHO clinical fact: HIV is the strongest
known risk factor for progression from latent to active TB, making co-testing
standard practice in high-burden African settings. Mirrors the
lassa-fever-symptoms-test.js precedent by honestly stating, in the FAQ, that TB
diagnostic testing (sputum, GeneXpert, chest X-ray) happens through hospital or
public TB-programme channels, not something BetterHealth Africa offers directly,
while still functioning as a useful educational resource on what those tests
involve. No specific TB mortality/incidence figures are stated; burden is
described qualitatively per the editorial caution around unstable/unconfirmed
numeric claims.

## Citable claim per H2
- Latent TB infection means the bacteria are present but contained by the immune system, while active TB disease means the bacteria are multiplying and making a person sick.
- The classic symptoms of active pulmonary TB are a cough lasting more than two to three weeks, night sweats, unexplained weight loss and fever, though these overlap with many other conditions and are not something to self-diagnose.
- Sputum smear microscopy and GeneXpert molecular testing are the standard diagnostic tests for active pulmonary TB, and both examine a sample of coughed-up sputum rather than blood.
- The tuberculin skin test (TST/Mantoux) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) detect whether a person has ever been infected with TB bacteria, not whether they currently have active disease.
- A chest X-ray can support a TB diagnosis by showing lung patterns typical of the disease, but it is a supportive tool used alongside sputum testing, not a standalone diagnostic test.
- HIV is the strongest known risk factor for progression from latent TB infection to active TB disease, which is why co-testing for both is standard practice across high-burden African health systems.
- Anyone with a cough lasting more than two to three weeks, unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or known contact with a person who has active TB should see a doctor for proper evaluation rather than waiting it out.
