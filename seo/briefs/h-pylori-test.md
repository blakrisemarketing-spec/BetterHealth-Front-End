## Citable claim per H2
- H. pylori is a spiral-shaped bacterium that lives in the mucus lining of the stomach and is the leading identifiable cause of stomach and duodenal ulcers worldwide.
- H. pylori spreads mainly through close person-to-person contact and contaminated food or water, and infection is commonly cited as very high across much of sub-Saharan Africa, often acquired in childhood.
- Most people with H. pylori have no symptoms at all, and when symptoms do appear they are largely the same regardless of sex: upper abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, and discomfort that is often worse on an empty stomach.
- Chronic H. pylori gastritis can reduce vitamin B12 absorption over time, because long-standing inflammation lowers stomach acid and intrinsic factor production, both of which the body needs to release and absorb B12 from food.
- Four main tests can detect H. pylori: a stool antigen test, a urea breath test, a blood antibody test, and biopsy taken during an endoscopy, and only the first two plus biopsy can confirm an active, current infection.
- H. pylori is classified as a Group 1 (definite) carcinogen for stomach cancer by the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer, a classification based on strength of association rather than individual risk, and the large majority of people infected with H. pylori never develop stomach cancer.
- H. pylori is treated with a supervised multi-drug regimen combining antibiotics with acid-suppressing medication, prescribed and monitored by a doctor rather than self-managed.
- A confirmed H. pylori infection is worth discussing with a doctor even without symptoms, because treatment decisions depend on your history, test type, and whether ulcer or alarm symptoms are present.
