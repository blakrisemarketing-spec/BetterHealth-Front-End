## Council Review Synthesis

This is a trust post, not a sales post. The reader has seen "doctor-reviewed" on health services before and quietly wonders whether it means anything. The job is to make the phrase concrete without overpromising.

Council diagnosis:
- Ogilvy on Advertising (Lead): trust comes from specifics, not adjectives. Do not just say "doctor-reviewed" — show what the review does and, just as importantly, what it does not replace. Honesty about the boundary is what makes the claim credible.
- Influence: authority and reassurance are the levers here. The reassurance must be earned by process detail (a review step, plain-language explanations, flags, a PDF you can take to your own doctor), not by tone alone.
- The Copywriter's Handbook: answer the objection the reader is already holding — "does this replace my own doctor?" Structure the carousel so each slide removes one piece of uncertainty and ends with a clear, low-pressure ask.
- Scientific Advertising: the measurable next step for a trust post is a question, not a booking. Make it genuinely easy to ask, and the comments/DMs become the proof and the next brief.
- Chair synthesis: define the phrase plainly, show the four concrete things the review gives you (plain-language explanations, out-of-range flags, an immediate notification for critical findings, a downloadable PDF), be explicit that it does not replace your own doctor, then invite a real question. Keep it calm and specific; specificity is the trust.

## Core Message

On BetterHealth, your result is reviewed by a doctor before it reaches your dashboard, so you are not left alone with a screen of confusing numbers. Each health indicator is explained in plain language, results outside the normal range are flagged, and for critical findings you get an immediate notification. You can also download a PDF report with your results, reference ranges, and explanations to use with your own doctor. Doctor-reviewed means your numbers are checked and explained clearly, not that any online step replaces a full consultation with your own healthcare provider.

## Clinical Guardrail

Do not describe the review step as a diagnosis, a treatment plan, or a substitute for the reader's own doctor. "Doctor-reviewed" here means the numbers are checked and explained in plain language before you see them, and that concerning results are flagged — not a full remote assessment of the person.

State only the verified facts: a doctor reviews results before they reach the dashboard; results land within 48 to 72 hours with each indicator explained in plain language; the platform flags results outside the normal range; critical findings trigger an immediate notification; you can download a PDF report (results + reference ranges + explanations) to take to your own doctor; Premium includes a doctor consultation. Discuss concerning results with a healthcare provider.

Do not invent doctor names, credentials, counts, review times per result, or accuracy claims. Do not imply the review replaces your own doctor or that it rules anything in or out.

Do not revive the retired fallback formulations about panic and clearer next steps, or about symptoms and diagnosis. The approved line "Don't panic. Seek clarity." is fine.

Preferred framing:
- "You are not left alone with confusing numbers."
- "Doctor-reviewed means your numbers are checked and explained — not a diagnosis on their own."
- "Each indicator is explained in plain language."
- "One result outside the reference range needs context before conclusions."
- "Take the PDF to your own doctor."
- "Don't panic. Seek clarity."

## Design Notes

- Primary visual mode: Explainer / Educational Carousel.
- Slide 1 is the scroll-stopper: the phrase "Doctor-reviewed results." in quotation marks with the question "What does that actually mean?" underneath. Clean, calm, high contrast — a question, not a claim.
- Use a simple process motif: a result card passing through a review step before it lands on a dashboard. Avoid stethoscope-and-white-coat clichés and any invented doctor faces or names.
- Keep imagery text-free when generated; add typography, logo, flags, and CTA as a composed overlay. For any human scene, use photorealistic Ghana-market realism (an ordinary person looking at a phone), not luxury stock.
- Slides get calmer after the hero. Slide 6 (the PDF you take to your own doctor) is the trust/save moment — make it visually distinct.
- Do not put real lab values, thresholds, or a fabricated sample report on the slides. If a mock dashboard is shown, keep indicators generic and blurred/illustrative.
- Keep the footer disclaimer on every educational slide: `General education only. For severe or urgent symptoms, seek medical care.`
- Ensure the "does not replace your own doctor" line is legible, not buried — it is load-bearing for trust.
- BetterHealth cream / sage / red accent palette, consistent with Days 2 and 4.

## Hypothesis

Framing the post as a plain question — "what does 'doctor-reviewed' actually mean?" — and then answering it with concrete, verifiable steps will build more trust than a generic reassurance claim. Because the CTA asks for a question rather than a booking, the post should generate comments and DMs that reveal the audience's real doubts about the results process, which become the next brief.
