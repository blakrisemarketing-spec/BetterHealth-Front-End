## Council Review Synthesis

The first version was medically careful, but too soft. It explained the test before earning the reader's attention.

Council diagnosis:
- Breakthrough Advertising: aim at the problem-aware reader, not only the education-aware reader. The reader is thinking, "I feel tired or unwell. Someone mentioned FBC. What does it actually tell me?"
- Tested Advertising Methods: the strongest save-worthy angle is "what it can show and what it does not check."
- Ogilvy on Advertising: add useful test detail, but keep it respectful and plain. Mention haemoglobin/haematocrit as related measures without turning the carousel into a lab manual.
- The Copywriter's Handbook: move the benefit earlier. Help the reader know what to ask next.
- Influence + Made to Stick: make the idea repeatable: "three blood clues, more context needed."
- Chair synthesis: open with why blood matters, then show why an FBC can be a useful starting test without making it sound like the answer to everything.

## Core Message

Your blood is constantly delivering oxygen and nutrients, carrying waste away, helping defend your body, and supporting clotting. That is why a Full Blood Count can be a useful starting test when something feels off: it looks at red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and related measures. It can help guide the next health question, but it does not check everything and should be read with symptoms, history, medicines, and other tests where needed.

## Clinical Guardrail

Do not present a Full Blood Count as a complete answer by itself. It can give useful clues, but results should be interpreted alongside symptoms, medical history, medicines, and other tests where needed.

Do not revive the retired fallback formulations about panic and clearer next steps, or about symptoms and diagnosis.

Preferred framing:
- "Your blood can give useful clues."
- "A Full Blood Count is a starting point, not the whole story."
- "Don't panic. Seek clarity."
- "Use the result to make a better health decision."
- "One result outside the reference range needs context before conclusions."

## Design Notes

- Keep the clean beige canvas and sage accent from Day 1.
- Use a simple explainer-card style, not medical stock imagery.
- Main slide idea: "Your blood can hold useful clues."
- Use large safe-zone text and avoid tiny lab-report details.
- Visual motif: three clear clue cards labelled oxygen delivery, immune response, and clotting support.
- Make Slide 1 visual and memorable: a simple "4-5 litres" blood fact, then the nutrient/oxygen transport idea.
- Make Slide 6 visually different because "what it does not check" is the save/share moment.
- Avoid making the post look like a hospital textbook; it should feel like practical health clarity for ordinary people.
