This post should make a confusing pair of kidney tests feel simple without turning into a nephrology lecture, and without ever printing a diagnostic stage on a slide.

Council diagnosis:
- The Copywriter's Handbook (Lead): use a clear two-part structure. Creatinine is the measured clue; eGFR is the estimate built from it. Lead with what the kidneys do, then define each term, then handle the objection ("does one result mean kidney disease?"). Make the CTA a simple Save.
- Tested Advertising Methods: the strongest save-worthy hook is not the test names. It is the plain-English relationship: "one is a waste product, the other is an estimate of how well your filter is working."
- Ogilvy on Advertising: trust comes from naming the limits. Say plainly that eGFR is an estimate and that muscle mass, hydration, and some medicines can shift these numbers.
- Scientific Advertising: the practical, measurable value is knowing what to ask next and that a single reading is read alongside repeat tests, symptoms, and history, not on its own.
- Made to Stick: the memorable line is "Creatinine is the clue. eGFR is the estimate of how well your filter is working."
- Chair synthesis: treat the carousel like a translation card for a lab form. Explain the kidneys' job, define creatinine and eGFR in one idea each, keep the "what it does not tell you by itself" slide honest, print no stage thresholds, and never imply one result diagnoses kidney disease.

## Core Message

Your kidneys work every minute to filter waste and extra fluid out of your blood. A creatinine test measures a waste product your muscles make and your kidneys filter out, so it can give a useful clue about filtering. eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) is an estimate of how well your kidneys are filtering, calculated from your creatinine along with factors like age and sex. Both can be useful clues, but eGFR is an estimate, not a perfect measure, and a single result needs context before conclusions.

## Clinical Guardrail

Do not present creatinine or eGFR as a diagnosis by itself in social copy. These give useful clues about kidney function and should be read with symptoms, health history, medicines, repeat tests, and other tests where needed.

Do not print diagnostic stage thresholds or CKD stage numbers on any slide. Do not imply that one result diagnoses kidney disease or that a normal result rules kidney disease out.

Frame eGFR clearly as an estimate. It is calculated, not directly measured, and can be affected by muscle mass, hydration, some medicines, a recent high-protein meal or supplements, pregnancy, and short-term changes, so one reading needs context.

Do not revive the retired fallback formulations about panic and clearer next steps, or about symptoms and diagnosis. The short approved line "Don't panic. Seek clarity." is fine.

Preferred framing:
- "Creatinine is the clue. eGFR is the estimate of how well your filter is working."
- "eGFR is an estimate, not a perfect measure."
- "One result needs context before conclusions."
- "Useful clues about kidney function, read with the whole picture."
- "A starting point, not the whole story."

## Instagram Caption

Your kidneys are quietly doing one of the most important jobs in your body: filtering waste and extra fluid out of your blood, every minute of every day.

So when a lab form mentions creatinine or eGFR, it is really asking one plain question: how well is that filter working?

Here is the simple version.

Creatinine is a waste product. Your muscles make it as part of normal daily activity, and healthy kidneys filter it out into your urine. Measuring how much is left in your blood can give a useful clue about how the filtering is going.

eGFR stands for estimated glomerular filtration rate. It is an estimate of how well your kidneys are filtering, calculated from your creatinine along with factors like your age and sex.

An easy way to remember it: creatinine is the clue, and eGFR is the estimate of how well your filter is working.

But notice the word estimate.

eGFR is an estimate, not a perfect measure. And creatinine can be affected by things like your muscle mass, how hydrated you are, and some medicines. That is why one reading is read with context, not on its own.

## Design Notes

- Primary visual mode: Explainer / Educational Carousel.
- Slide 1 should be scroll-stopping but clean: the idea that the kidneys filter the whole blood supply many times a day, then the two words creatinine and eGFR.
- Use a simple filter motif (a clean funnel or filter graphic), not medical stock imagery or a hospital-textbook diagram.

- Do NOT put diagnostic thresholds, eGFR cut-off numbers, or CKD stage numbers on any slide. Keep language qualitative. If numeric context is ever needed, it belongs in caption copy with clinical framing, not on the slides.
- Use large safe-zone text and avoid tiny lab-report details.
