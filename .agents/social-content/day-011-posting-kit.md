## Council Review Synthesis

This post should feel like a keepsake checklist, not a lecture. The value is a short, memorable list people want to keep and pass on, with every number framed as a starting point that needs clinical context.

Council diagnosis:
- Scientific Advertising (Lead): the reason to save is a clear, useful shortlist and one measurable next step. Do not pad with statistics we cannot cite; the "reason why" is that these numbers are quiet and often unnoticed until checked, so knowing them early gives you and a clinician something to work with.
- Tested Advertising Methods: the strongest hook is the "before you feel sick" tension. Many of these numbers give few or no symptoms early, so the first slide should sell curiosity, not fear.
- The Copywriter's Handbook: use a strict one-number-per-slide structure. Each slide answers the same two questions in plain English: what it is, and why it is worth knowing. Predictable structure makes it easy to save and re-read.
- Made to Stick: make the set concrete and countable. "Five numbers" is simple and repeatable; a person should be able to recall the list after one pass.
- Chair synthesis: build a calm, numbered carousel of five numbers. Explain each simply, keep every one hedged, and never print diagnostic thresholds or imply a single reading confirms or rules out disease. Close with the one action: talk these through with a clinician, and save/share the list.

Material disagreement kept: Tested Advertising Methods pushed for a sharper "silent risk" hook; the Chair kept the hook curiosity-led rather than fear-led per the copy rules, so the tension is framed as "worth knowing early," not "danger."

## Core Message

Some of the most useful health numbers are quiet. They can drift for a while before you feel anything, which is why knowing them early can help. Blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, body weight or BMI, and a kidney or blood marker are five numbers worth knowing and discussing with a clinician. None of them is a diagnosis on its own. Each is a useful clue that needs context, read with your symptoms, history, medicines, and other tests where needed.

## Clinical Guardrail

Do not present any single number as a diagnosis or as proof of a disease. Each of these five numbers is a starting point that needs clinical context, not a verdict.

Do not print specific diagnostic thresholds or reference ranges on the slides (no "normal is X", no cut-off values). If numbers are ever needed later, they belong in caption copy with clinical context, not on the visual.

Do not turn body weight or BMI into shame. Frame BMI as a rough screening clue, not a measure of worth or health on its own; note it does not distinguish muscle from fat and is one input among several.

Do not revive the retired fallback formulations about panic and clearer next steps, or about symptoms and diagnosis. The approved line "Don't panic. Seek clarity." is fine.

Preferred framing:
- "Numbers worth knowing, not numbers to fear."
- "A useful clue, not a diagnosis."
- "One number outside a range needs context before conclusions."
- "BMI is a rough screen, not a verdict."
- "Worth knowing, and worth discussing with a clinician."
- "Don't panic. Seek clarity."

## Instagram Caption

Some of the most useful health numbers are quiet.

They can drift for a while before you feel anything. That is exactly why knowing them early can help, and why they are worth discussing with a clinician before something feels wrong.

Here are five numbers worth knowing:

1. Blood pressure
The force of blood pushing against your artery walls. It often gives few signs early, so knowing it can help you and a clinician notice changes sooner.

2. Blood sugar
The level of sugar (glucose) in your blood, checked by fasting glucose or HbA1c. It can change quietly, and knowing it helps a clinician review how your body is handling sugar over time.

3. Cholesterol (lipid profile)
A group of fats in your blood, including different types of cholesterol. It usually causes no symptoms you can feel, so the numbers give useful clues worth talking through.

4. Body weight or BMI
BMI is a rough screen that compares weight to height. It is one clue among several, not a verdict, and not a measure of your worth. It does not tell muscle from fat, so read it gently and with a clinician.

5. A kidney marker (creatinine / eGFR)
A blood test that gives a picture of how your kidneys are working. Kidneys can change quietly, so this marker is a useful clue worth reviewing alongside your other results.

Important: none of these is a diagnosis on its own. One number outside a range needs context before conclusions. Read each with your symptoms, history, medicines, and any other tests your clinician recommends.

Don't panic. Seek clarity.

## Design Notes

- Primary visual mode: Explainer / Educational Carousel.
- Slide 1 is the scroll-stopper: a clean "5 numbers worth knowing before you feel sick" hero, calm and curiosity-led, not fear-led.
- One number per slide for slides 2 to 6, each with the same two-line structure ("What it is" / "Why it is worth knowing"). Predictable layout makes it easy to save and re-read.
- Do NOT put diagnostic thresholds, cut-offs, or reference ranges on any slide (no "normal is X"). Keep the copy qualitative.
- If any decorative data viz is used (e.g. a small illustrative gauge, droplet, or trend line), keep it internally correct and clearly decorative, and do not label it with real threshold numbers that could be read as a clinical range.
- Frame Slide 5 (body weight / BMI) with visible warmth: neutral palette, no scales-as-judgement imagery, no before/after tropes. Reinforce "a rough screen, not a verdict."
- Use the BetterHealth cream canvas with sage and red accents; keep large safe-zone text.
- Slide 7 is the calm close: "none is a diagnosis on its own" plus the BetterHealth positioning and CTA.
- Keep the footer disclaimer on every educational slide: `General education only. For severe or urgent symptoms, seek medical care.`
- Ensure the page indicator, logo, disclaimer, and CTA stay readable and inside platform safe zones.
