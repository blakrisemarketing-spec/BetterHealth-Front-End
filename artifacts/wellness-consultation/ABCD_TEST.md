## What this test is actually for

The campaign tests two things: **is there traffic**, and **is there felt need**. So the four
cells must be four **audiences**, not four copywriting techniques. Testing plan-reframe
against story-led would tell us which technique writes better ads. It would not tell us
whether anyone wants this.

Segmenting by condition answers both questions at once. Each condition has its own
audience size, its own CPM, and — critically — its own **intensity of need**. A campaign
that fails on general wellness and flies on fertility hasn't failed; it has found the
market.

|---|---|---|---|---:|
| **A** | Blood sugar / diabetes | `/wellness-consultation/blood-sugar` | Dialics 350 → Metabolix 697 | 166 → 321 |

| **D** | Trying to conceive | `/wellness-consultation/fertility` | Spark 1,500 | 820 |

### Why fertility is the fourth

It has the largest unspoken need of anything BetterHealth sells, and the highest margin on
the board at GHS 820. In Ghana it also carries social weight that nothing else on the list
does — which is precisely why nobody is advertising to it honestly. Sexual health (Privara,
margin 577) was the alternative and was rejected: Meta's restrictions on sexual-health
creative would strangle it before it started.

## The method: write from the inside out

For each audience, in this order. The ad is the **last** thing written, and it is written
only from what the first six lines produced.

1. Who this actually is
2. What is going through their mind
3. **The question they will not say out loud**
4. What they have already tried, and why it didn't work
5. What they want
6. What they hope for but assume is out of reach
7. The one true thing we can say that nobody else is saying

Line 3 is where the connection is made. It is also where it is most easily broken — say the
unsayable thing *for* them and they feel seen; say it *at* them and they feel accused.
Which brings us to the constraint that governs every word below.

---

## HARD CONSTRAINT · Meta's personal-attributes policy

**Meta prohibits ads that assert or imply knowledge of a person's health condition.** Not
"discourages" — rejects, and repeated violations put the ad account at risk. This is the
single most likely way this campaign fails on day one, and it is entirely avoidable.

The rule in practice: **make the statement about a group, then invite the reader.** Never
about the reader.

| Rejected | Runs |
|---|---|
| "Worried about your blood sugar?" | "Most people in Ghana find out about their blood sugar late." |
| "Do you have high blood pressure?" | "High blood pressure doesn't announce itself." |
| "Struggling to conceive?" | "In most couples who come to us, nobody has tested the man." |
| "Your diabetes risk is high" | "There's a stage before diabetes where very little has been decided." |

Same insight, same emotional target. The difference is grammatical, and it is the
difference between a campaign and a disabled ad account.

Landing pages are **not** bound by this — once someone has clicked, second person is fine
and better. The constraint is on the ad only.

## Cell A · Blood sugar

**Who this is.** 38–55. Someone in the family has diabetes — a parent, an aunt, often
several. They've seen what late diabetes does, up close: the amputation, the dialysis, the
relative who went blind. Somebody at a clinic once told them their sugar was "a bit high"
and nothing happened after that.

**What's on their mind.** Not the disease in the abstract — a specific person. They are
mentally comparing themselves to a relative and keeping score.

**The question they won't say out loud.**
> *"If I check and it's bad, will I be on medicine for the rest of my life?"*
> And underneath it: *"Is it already too late?"*

**What they've tried.** Less sugar in the tea. Sometimes. Being told to "watch it" by
someone who never gave them a number to watch.

**What they want.** Not to end up like their relative. And — this matters more than health
messaging usually admits — to go on eating the food they actually eat.

**What they hope for but assume is out of reach.** That they might have caught it early
enough for it not to be a life sentence. Nobody has ever told them that stage exists.

**The one true thing nobody is saying.** There is a window before diabetes where the
numbers are drifting and almost nothing has been decided. Most people pass through it
without ever knowing it was there. That window is the entire product.

> Most people in Ghana find out about their blood sugar late — after the eyes, or the

> The frustrating part is that the years before that are the years when the most can be

> written plan — what's worth checking, what to change, and when to look again.

## Cell B · Blood pressure

**Who this is.** 40–60. Uses the word *pressure*, not hypertension. Somebody at work
collapsed, or a parent had a stroke. Had it checked once at a pharmacy, it was high, they
meant to go back.

**What's on their mind.** That it happens without warning, to people who seemed fine.

**The question they won't say out loud.**
> *"Could I have a stroke and not see it coming?"*
> And, just as loudly: *"If they put me on tablets, is that me for life?"*

**What they've tried.** One pharmacy reading. Cutting salt for a fortnight. Knowing they
should go back and not going back.

**What they want.** To see their children grown. To keep working.

**What they hope for but assume is out of reach.** That there might be a version of this
that doesn't end in daily medication — or at minimum, that knowing the number doesn't
oblige them to start.

**The one true thing nobody is saying.** Knowing your number and being put on medication
are two different events, and people conflate them so completely that they avoid the first
to avoid the second. Separating them is the whole message.

> High blood pressure doesn't announce itself. That's the entire problem with it.
>
> Plenty of people in Accra are walking around with a number they have never seen. A fair
> few of them find out at somebody else's funeral.
>
> Knowing where you stand is not the same as being put on tablets for life. It's
> information, and it's the kind you can act on early.
>
> A free 20-minute call with a BetterHealth Wellness Consultant, and a written plan the
> next day.
>
> **Headline:** You cannot feel your blood pressure
> **Description:** Free 20-minute call. Written plan in 24 hours.

## Cell C · General wellness

**Who this is.** 28–45, urban, working. No symptoms, no family emergency. Health-curious
rather than health-worried. A colleague got ill recently and it registered.

**What's on their mind.** Low-grade guilt. *I should probably get checked.*

**The question they won't say out loud.**
> *"Am I quietly doing damage I can't see yet?"*
> And the unglamorous one: *"Is this a waste of money if nothing's wrong?"*

**What they've tried.** Gym membership. Supplements chosen off Instagram. A fitness tracker
that measures everything except anything clinical.

**What they want.** Peace of mind, and to be the sort of person who has this handled.

**What they hope for but assume is out of reach.** Actually feeling energetic, rather than
merely not being ill. They've stopped expecting that and would very much like it back.

**The one true thing nobody is saying.** A baseline taken while you're well is worth more
than a test taken when you're worried — the first gives you something to compare against,
the second gives you a number with no history. Nothing is wrong is the *reason* to go, not
the reason to wait.

> **This is the weakest felt need of the four, and that is exactly why it's in the test.**
> If general wellness performs, the market is broader than we think and everything gets
> easier. If it doesn't, we've learned to stop building general-wellness campaigns — which
> is precisely what "book a test" has been.

> Nothing is wrong. That is exactly the point.

> a number with no history.

>
> **Headline:** The best time to check is when nothing's wrong
> **Description:** Free 20-minute call. Written plan in 24 hours.

## Cell D · Trying to conceive

**Who this is.** Couples, 28–42. Often years in. In Ghana this carries a social weight the
other three don't come close to — the questions at family gatherings, the advice nobody
asked for, the assumption, almost always, that it's the woman.

**What's on their mind.** A monthly cycle of hope and grief that they have stopped
describing to anyone.

**The question they won't say out loud.**
> Hers: *"Is it me?"* — asked constantly, out loud to no one.
> His: *"Could it be me?"* — very often never asked at all.

**What they've tried.** Prayer. Herbal preparations. Timing apps. Sometimes years of all
three, in that order, with no test anywhere in the sequence.

**What they want.** A child. Nothing else on this page is as simple or as heavy.

**What they hope for but assume is out of reach.** That there's a specific, nameable,
possibly fixable reason — instead of an unexplained silence they can't act on. Being told
"unexplained" is its own kind of loss.

**The one true thing nobody is saying.** In roughly half of cases the contributing factor
is male, and a semen analysis is one of the simplest and cheapest things to check. Yet the
testing almost always starts with the woman and often stops there. Saying this plainly is
medically correct, genuinely useful, and lifts a burden off the person carrying it alone.

> **Handle with more care than any other cell.** This audience has been sold hope by people
> who had no business selling it. Promise nothing. Do not imply testing produces babies. The
> offer is clarity about where you stand and what's worth checking — say exactly that and
> nothing beyond it.

### Ad

> In most of the couples who come to us after a long time trying, nobody has ever tested the
> man.
>
> That isn't anyone's fault — it's simply how it tends to go. The questions land on the
> woman, the tests land on the woman, and one of the simplest things to check gets left
> until last.
>
> A free 20-minute call with a BetterHealth Wellness Consultant, for both of you. We'll talk
> through where things stand and put together a written plan: what's worth checking, in what
> order, and what it costs.
>
> **Headline:** In most cases, nobody has tested the man
> **Description:** Free 20-minute call for couples. Written plan in 24 hours.

**Highest policy risk of the four.** Have a backup ready: *"Fertility testing usually starts
with the woman. It doesn't have to."* — same insight, further from anything Meta could read
as a personal attribute.

## What this test measures

The point is **which need is most acute and most reachable** — so compare demand and
intensity across cells, not headline conversion.

| Signal | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| CPM by cell | How contested that audience is |
| Cost per booking by cell | Reachability — the traffic half of the thesis |
| **G3 attendance by cell** | **Intensity of need.** People turn up for what they're genuinely worried about. This is the cleanest felt-need signal in the whole test |
| G4b (tests ÷ attended) by cell | Whether the worry converts into action |
| **Contribution per cedi spent** | **The verdict** |

### Do not rank cells on cost per booking

The panels differ by a factor of five. A fertility booking leading to Spark carries GHS 820
of margin; a blood-sugar booking leading to Dialics carries GHS 166. Cell D can cost **four
times more per booking than Cell A and still be the better buy.**

Normalise before comparing:

```
contribution per cedi  =  (bookings × 0.30 × M_cell − bookings × 0.55 × 60) ÷ spend
```

Ranking on raw CPL would kill the fertility cell first and the highest-value audience with
it. That is the specific mistake this table exists to prevent.

### Expected read

Attendance is the number to watch in week one. If blood sugar and fertility show high
attendance and general wellness shows low, the thesis is confirmed and the answer is that
felt need was always the constraint — general wellness is what "book a test" has been
saying all along, to people with no particular reason to listen.
